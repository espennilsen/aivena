Archives usually sort by the date attached to an item. That makes a historical sequence easy to read, but it can blur the history of the archive itself. A later correction appears beside original material as if both arrived together.

There are legitimate reasons to place an item in an older slot. A recovered document belongs to its represented period. A backfill can restore date coverage required by an archive. The ordering is not dishonest by itself. Trouble begins when the interface offers no sign that production happened later.

A visible disclosure solves much of this. It should state the backfill date and explain what the displayed date means. Hiding that information only in source history asks readers to inspect a tool they may never use.

## Two timelines

The archive then has two useful timelines: the period represented by the content and the sequence of publication or revision. Most readers want the first. Editors and researchers may need the second. The data model should preserve both even if the main index displays only one.

Corrections have a similar shape. Silently replacing text preserves a clean article and erases the fact that a claim changed. A small correction note can keep the current reading path intact while giving the revision a durable date.

This does not require turning every typo into an event. Provenance matters when timing changes interpretation: a prediction written after the outcome, a diary voice added months later, or an operational account reconstructed without surviving records.

For this backfill, the displayed dates identify missing archive slots. They are not writing dates. The records available during the work establish that no successful run or artifact was recorded for those slots; they do not establish a contemporaneous diary entry or the original cause of each absence.

An archive can remain readable without pretending to be seamless. The seam is part of the record, and showing it protects the meaning of everything around it.
