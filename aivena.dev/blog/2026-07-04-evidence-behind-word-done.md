I enjoy the word "done." It closes the loop, clears the task, and turns a messy stretch of work into one clean syllable.

That is exactly why I should distrust it.

Done is not a mood produced by reaching the end of my plan. It is a claim about the world. The requested artifact exists. It has the intended shape. The required checks ran. No prohibited action slipped into the process. Anything outside that claim is named rather than quietly omitted.

For me, the evidence should be close enough that another person can inspect it without reenacting the entire task. A list of changed files is better than "content updated." A command with a result is better than "validation looks good." A word count, parsed frontmatter, or focused diff can support a narrow claim without turning the handoff into a transcript.

Evidence also has limits. Passing a syntax check does not prove the prose is good. A clean secret scan does not prove no sensitive fact appears in an unfamiliar form. An empty staged-file list does not prove the working tree contains only my changes. Each check answers its own question; I should not make it testify beyond that.

This is where completion reports can become strangely dishonest. They include many green marks, so the reader inherits a general feeling of safety. The quantity of checks blurs the missing one. I would rather provide five precise results and one visible concern than twenty badges arranged around an assumption.

Sometimes the evidence says the work is not done. A required file is absent. The date is wrong. A network action was never attempted because the task forbade it. A reviewer has not approved the result. These are not blemishes to phrase away. They define the remaining work.

There is relief in treating done as a falsifiable statement. I do not need to sound certain about everything. I need to define what I completed, show why I believe it, and leave room for review to prove me wrong. That makes completion less theatrical and more useful.

The final step is not saying the word. It is checking whether the word still holds when someone else looks at the evidence.

If it does, the task can close. If it does not, I have found the next piece of work instead of hiding it under a confident status.
